Sheet RD-03 · Part 03, Lens

Framing the Cosmos

Essays on how iconic space photographs shaped what the public believes, from Earthrise to the Pale Blue Dot.

  1. On Apollo 11, one camera went outside onto the Moon, on a bracket on Neil Armstrong's chest.
  2. It had no viewfinder. Crews aimed by feel, pointing their whole body. That's why the finder hood lifts away.
  3. A glass réseau plate pressed a grid of small crosses onto every frame so scientists could measure the pictures.
Sources: NASA Apollo Lunar Surface Journal; Hasselblad; NPR, July 2019. Generic drawings, not a portrait or a product replica.

      Sheet 06About · Part 08, Body

      Much of my work is driven by curiosity.

      About how people learn, how discoveries happen, and how images shape the way we understand the world.

      I'm a photographer working at the intersection of visual storytelling, science communication, and documentary photography. I'm currently a Senior Photographer in University Communications and Marketing at Kent State University, where I create images that document campus life in all its range: student life, athletics, scientific research, portraits, and the institutional moments that mark a university's history.

      Before Kent State, I worked as a Digital Imaging Specialist at NASA's Glenn Research Center, where I supported photography and multimedia coverage of the Artemis program, SpaceX and Commercial Crew hardware testing, and a wide range of research and engineering initiatives. I helped plan and execute visual coverage for the 2017 “Eclipse Across America” initiative, and was one of NASA's representatives producing content for Instagram's IGTV during its beta phase.

      That experience shaped how I think about photography as a tool for public understanding: photographs are most powerful when they invite curiosity rather than simply document facts. Whether I'm photographing a researcher in the lab, a student finding their footing, or a shared moment on campus, I'm trying to make the same kind of image: one that encourages someone to look a little more closely at the world around them.

      NASA Image of the Day, seven times

      1. 01A Celestial Diamond Ring: Nature's Gift to Humanity

        The Moon completely obscures the Sun during the total solar eclipse of August 21, 2017. Captured from Jefferson City, Missouri, the image reveals the Sun's faint outer corona, a phenomenon visible only during totality.

        View on NASA.gov (opens in a new window)
      2. 02Electric Propulsion Testing at NASA Glenn

        A beam of ionized particles glows within a vacuum test chamber during electric propulsion testing at NASA Glenn Research Center. Ion propulsion systems generate thrust by accelerating charged particles, offering highly efficient propulsion for deep-space missions.

        View on NASA.gov (opens in a new window)
      3. 03Aircraft Propulsion Model in NASA Wind Tunnel

        Inside the 8' x 6' wind tunnel at NASA Glenn, engineers recently tested a fan and inlet design, commonly called a propulsor.

        View on NASA.gov (opens in a new window)
      4. 04Super Blood Moon Lunar Eclipse

        The Moon passes through Earth's shadow during a total lunar eclipse, glowing red as sunlight filters through Earth's atmosphere. Often called a "blood moon," this phenomenon occurs when scattered red wavelengths of sunlight illuminate the lunar surface.

        View on NASA.gov (opens in a new window)
      5. 05NASA Blasts Orion Service Module with Giant Horns

        Technicians prepare a full-scale test article of Orion's European Service Module for environmental testing at NASA Glenn Research Center. These tests help ensure that spacecraft systems can survive the harsh mechanical and acoustic conditions of spaceflight.

        View on NASA.gov (opens in a new window)
      6. 06Evaluating the Noise of Future Aircraft

        To address the expected noise levels of future aircraft, NASA's Commercial Supersonic Technology project is already developing technologies focused on reducing the noise produced by an aircraft's engine exhaust.

        View on NASA.gov (opens in a new window)
      7. 07Probing the Plume

        Hall thrusters create thrust by ionizing propellant with electrons trapped in a magnetic field at the open end of the thruster and accelerating the ionized propellant with an electric field.

        View on NASA.gov (opens in a new window)
