      What I would bring to a communications team.

      Gold marks what a tool did. Red marks where a person decided.

      How I lead change

      AI should make a communications team better at the work it already does. It shouldn't replace the people doing that work, and it shouldn't arrive as a mandate. Here's how I bring it in.

      • Test on real work first. I try a tool on my own assignments before asking anyone else to change how they work. If it doesn't hold up on deadline, I don't recommend it.
      • People stay in charge. The tool suggests and a person decides. Every workflow I share marks the point where someone checks the output before it goes anywhere.
      • Start with the tedious parts. Colleagues adopt what gives them time back, so I begin with metadata and transcription, not the creative work they care most about.
      • Compare before adding. Sometimes the better answer is a feature we already pay for. I weigh a new tool against what we have, and I measure once it's part of the routine.

      One decision I owned

      When I started on AI training for communicators, the expected version would have taught people to get more out of a chatbot. I went the other way. For a university newsroom, the real risk isn't slow writing. It's a quote that sounds right and isn't. So I designed Prompt to Publish and its workshop around checking: a 90-minute session where communicators test AI-generated quotes against their sources, a knowledge check at the end, and a plan to measure whether people actually change how they verify. Trust comes from checking, so that's where the training starts.

      The night sky, from 1,100 miles away

      Every deep-sky image: stacked in Siril, color calibrated against Gaia star data. Exposure is given as frames x seconds, then total time. Telescope time from Starfront Observatories (opens in a new window).

      How a night works

      1. PlanCheck the roof, weather and Moon. A bright Moon means emission nebulae with the light-pollution filter; dark nights are for galaxies.
      2. CaptureA plan in the Seestar app runs the targets unattended, saving every 10 to 30 second frame.
      3. StackHundreds of frames are aligned and combined in Siril, then color calibrated against Gaia star data.
      4. FinishA gentle stretch, then tone and crop in Lightroom Classic.
      5. Fly-inThe photo's own stars are set at depth in Blender and a camera pushes in, ending on the real still. Always labeled.
      6. PublishCaptions with distance, exposure and sources, and alt text for every image.

      Who did what

      I chose the targets, the framing and every aesthetic call. Claude did the research, the scheduling math and the checklists for each night, and ran most of the stacking in Siril on my Mac while I reviewed and chose between versions. I processed the Heart Nebula myself with Claude coaching. The fly-in tool was built with Claude for this project.

      Connected work

      I have made one of these images before. In 2017 I was the sole photographer on NASA Glenn's coverage of the total eclipse, and the diamond ring ran as NASA Image of the Day. Eclipse Across America

      Sheet 06About · P08, Body

      Much of my work is driven by curiosity.

      About how people learn, how discoveries happen, and how images shape the way we understand the world.

      I help communication teams adopt AI in ways that make their work stronger and keep people in charge of it.

      At Kent State's University Communications and Marketing, I'm the senior photographer and, informally, the person colleagues come to about AI. Leadership gave me room to try tools on our real work, so that's where every recommendation starts. When something holds up, I write down how to use it, where a person has to check it, and when to leave it alone. Then I share it one colleague at a time, beginning with the tools that save the most effort. The meeting transcription tool I tested first is now part of a colleague's routine too.

      Right now that work includes an archive workflow that uses Claude to strengthen metadata across thousands of UCM images, a field guide and workshop kit on checking AI-generated quotes, and a panel at the UPAA symposium in June 2026 on how AI is changing the role of photographers and communications teams.

      The foundation is twenty years of photography and video: four years as a digital imaging specialist at NASA Glenn Research Center on the Artemis and Commercial Crew programs, freelance work for the Associated Press, AFP and Cleveland Magazine, and Framing the Cosmos, my writing on how space images shape what the public believes. That background is why I approach AI the way I do. I know how long it takes to earn an audience's trust in an image, and how fast it can go.

        A beam of ionized particles glows within a vacuum test chamber during electric propulsion testing at NASA Glenn Research Center. Ion propulsion systems generate thrust by accelerating charged particles, offering highly efficient propulsion for deep-space missions.
